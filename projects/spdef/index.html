Knot count as a regularizer

HyperNeRF americano · casual hand-held capture · 200 frames · 8.1 MB

The unreliable case, where the input barely constrains the motion. Cutting the number of spline knots removes degrees of freedom from the trajectory, which stabilizes the reconstruction much as the loss terms do, at the cost of some high-frequency detail. The third panel shows what the full knot count does with no regularizers at all.

These three are rendered square and wider than the capture camera, which was a 536×960 portrait frame. You are seeing well outside the filmed frustum, floaters included.

The spline itself, between the supervised frames

DeformingThings4D astra · samba dancing · 837 frames · 7,921 points · 5.0 MB for the whole sequence, 0.2 MB of it up front

The two cards above render gaussians. This one drops the rasterizer entirely and fits the same spline deformation field straight to a mesh’s vertices, which is how the field’s temporal quality is measured. What ships is not a replay: it is the fitted spline, a per-point offset and tangent at each knot, and your browser evaluates the same cubic Hermite the field itself uses. The mesh is decimated ≈4×, from the sequence’s 31,515 vertices down to 7,921 to stay light.

What the model actually stores

D-NeRF bouncingballs · 14,534 canonical gaussians · three 128×128×32 tri-planes · 1,500 of them sampled at all 75 knots · 2.4 MB

A plane at time t is base + Σr wr(t)·resr: one fixed plane plus six more, scaled by six numbers. Press play and watch what moves: the canonical cloud stays exactly where it is, and the sheets change. The plane axes are the world axes, and each sheet cell sits where the field samples it, which is why a sheet reads as the cloud’s shadow on that wall. Switch on cast to the planes to draw the lookup itself: the line from a gaussian’s centre to the foot of its perpendicular on each face is the address the field is read at, and the sheet colour where it lands is the value that comes back. Hard ellipsoids turns the soft splats into the points they actually are; shrink the radius to pull them apart. Beside it is the same field’s output: a sample of those gaussians pushed to the two knots the playhead sits between, the tangent each one leaves its knot with, and the arc the renderer evaluates in between.

Want the planes flat, with the six weight curves and the other ways of reading them: the template, each residual on its own, and where the field does most of its work? Open the tri-plane inspector.

The two gaussian comparisons share a one-time ~31 MB decoder, cached by your browser after the first load; the trajectory demo and the tri-plane figure need no decoder at all: the first streams its knots as you scrub, the second evaluates its planes on the GPU from six numbers per frame. Each card loads only when you ask for it, and clear gives it back (scenes, renderers and decoded motion) if you would rather not hold them all at once. Once a card is loaded, almost every panel works the same way: drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom, shift-drag or two-finger drag to pan. That is worth doing in the knot panel, where two consecutive knots are a fraction of a pixel apart until you go in. The one exception is the americano comparison, a close hand-held capture you can fly through instead: scroll there changes speed, W/A/S/D and Q/E move the focused panel, and on a touch device an on-screen stick and up/down buttons appear on its first panel. Within a card the cameras are linked, so whichever you move, the others follow.
