TempFormer

A video denoiser sees a few frames at a time. Here is why that makes it flicker, and what fixes it.

Mingyang Song1, Yang Zhang2, Tunç O. Aydın2 1ETH Zürich, Switzerland    2DisneyResearch|Studios, Switzerland

The seam

TempFormer denoises video five frames at a time and returns only the middle three, then slides along by two. Each bottle is a frame: its colour is the frame's content, the jitter between neighbours is the noise, and the ribbon underneath is what the sequence should have been. Each outlet pours a weighted mixture of all five inlets, and those weights are not chosen but fitted: least squares over a few hundred sequences like this one, re-solved every time you move a control.

Two consecutive positions of that one machine: window A takes bottles 0–4 and pours slots 1, 2, 3; window B takes bottles 2–6 and pours slots 3, 4, 5. The same machine and the same weights both times, two frames apart, which is what sliding means. But both of them pour slot 3.

They will not agree. Window A sees slot 3 at the very edge of what it can read, with no frame after it; window B sees the same slot comfortably in the middle. Same bottle, same water, different mixtures. In the finished video only one pour survives, so the sequence steps sideways exactly where the windows change over. That step is the flicker, and it is what the paper is about: a sliding block strategy with recurrent architecture, and a new loss term, the Overlap Loss.

—ΔE

Seam. How far apart the two pours of slot 3 are, and the quantity the Overlap Loss minimises. The bar is that gap against the same scene with both controls off.

—ΔE

Colour error. Mean distance from the delivered five slots to the true ribbon, which is the accuracy the seam is bought with.
