Digital Camera Noise Synthesis

What a camera’s grain actually looks like — measured, and put back on your own photographs.

Mingyang Song1,2, Yang Zhang2, Tunç O. Aydın2, Elham Amin Mansour1,2, Christopher Schroers2 1ETH Zürich, Switzerland    2DisneyResearch|Studios, Switzerland

Playground

Sensor noise is not the white Gaussian speckle that denoising papers used to assume. It is coloured — brighter in the shadows than in the highlights, unequal across R, G and B, correlated between neighbouring pixels by the camera’s own processing, and correlated between the three channels as well. Pick an image below and it is regrained as one of five smartphones at one of fourteen ISO levels, then drag the divider to compare against the clean original.

What runs here is not the network. These presets are measured straight off SIDD’s paired clean and noisy captures — the same photographs the generator was trained on, read one step earlier — and reduced to three things: how loud the grain is at each clean intensity, how strongly neighbouring pixels agree, and how the three channels move together. That is what the page ships: 70 presets of summary statistics, about 180 KB of numbers.

SIDD holds 34 of those 70 camera-and-ISO combinations. The other 36 are interpolated across ISO from the ones it does hold, and are labelled so in the picker — a fitted curve between two readings is a useful guess, but it is not a reading.

At 1.00× the preset is reproduced exactly as measured, so what you see is the model’s own answer for that camera at that ISO; the slider scales the finished noise field without reshaping any of it, which is how you can push past a real ISO and still be looking at the right kind of grain. Grain ×4 shows the noise field on its own, amplified four times about mid-grey; Seed draws a different realisation of the same distribution.

The model

nc = kc ∗ ( σc(Ic) · wc )     noisy = clip(I + n, 0, 1) w is unit Gaussian noise, correlated across channels by a 3×3 Cholesky factor; σc is a 17-knot lookup evaluated at the clean intensity; kc is a 9×9 symmetric kernel supplying the spatial correlation, normalised so that Σk² = 1 — convolution then preserves exactly the variance the curve asked for.

Two of the three ingredients are plotted above, and neither is what the textbook model would predict: the curve is not affine in intensity, and the kernel is not a blur. The paper is where the generative model they summarise is derived and evaluated.

The third does not plot, and it is the one that is easiest to get wrong. Because the channels are mixed before each is filtered by its own kernel, the correlation left in the finished grain is not the correlation the model needs: it comes out attenuated by ⟨kc, kd⟩, which is below 1 whenever two channels’ kernels differ at all. Measuring the output and using it directly would under-colour the grain by exactly that factor, so it is divided back out before the matrix is projected to the nearest valid correlation matrix and factored.

Datasets & credits

The presets are measured from an open-source smartphone-noise dataset; the imagery on and around this page is credited alongside it.

SIDD — real clean/noisy smartphone pairs, and the source of every preset above (G4, GP, IP, N6 and S6 are its five cameras). Its ground truth is a paired capture rather than a denoiser’s output, which is what lets the grain be read off the difference without inheriting a denoiser’s errors. The Medium sRGB split is what was measured — 160 scenes over 34 camera-and-ISO combinations.
